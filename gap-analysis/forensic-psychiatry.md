# Gap Analysis: mrc-psych-lake (web) vs SPMM Source PDFs
## Forensic Psychiatry — What's Missing, From an Exam Standpoint

**Purpose of this document:** This compares every fact in the two SPMM Forensic Psychiatry
source books (`12-1-forensic-psychiatry-1.pdf`, `12-1-forensic-psychiatry-2.pdf`, extracted in
full into `content/notes/forensic-psychiatry.json`) against what is actually rendered on the
live web page (`content/study-guides/forensic-psychiatry.json`, 27 sections). The goal is to
find exam-relevant material — named studies, tables, trap facts, exact figures — that a student
relying solely on the web page would never see, even though it exists in the source PDFs they
paid for and expect to be fully condensed.

**Overall context**: this is one of the stronger guides in the corpus — 27 sections, extensive
use of `trap`/`mnemonic`/`table` blocks pulled from the question bank, and good numeric fidelity
on everything it does cover (no numerical discrepancies were found between the book text and the
web page — every figure cross-checked matched exactly). The gaps below are real but narrower than
a "half-empty guide" problem: they cluster around (a) one entirely missing named epidemiological
study, (b) several sub-classification systems/tables mentioned in passing in the books but never
surfaced, and (c) a handful of specific headline statistics dropped when a paragraph was
condensed.

---

## Section-by-Section Gap Findings

### 1. Relationship Between Crime and Mental Disorder (book 1, p.2)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing:**
- The three-approaches framework the book opens with (Walsh 2002) for studying the MI-violence
  link — (i) prevalence of violent acts in those with mental illness, (ii) prevalence of mental
  illness in those who've committed violent acts, (iii) prevalence of violence in the community
  regardless of mental health status. This is the conceptual scaffold the whole topic sits on and
  is a plausible "which of the following is NOT one of the three approaches" EMI/SBA stem — it
  doesn't appear anywhere in the web page.
- **99.97%** — the book's own counter-framing of the schizophrenia-homicide risk figures:
  "Note that 99.97% of those with schizophrenia would not be convicted of serious violence in any
  given year." The web page keeps the 1:3000 (men) / 1:33,000 (women) annual homicide-risk figures
  but drops this specific counter-statistic, which is exactly the kind of "don't overweight a
  small absolute risk" trap MRCPsych favours.
- Hodgins et al. (2004): mental illness (especially psychosis) is more common in the **remanded**
  population who were violent vs. non-violent — a specific named finding, entirely absent.

### 2. Fazel & Grann (2006) — Population Attributable Risk of Schizophrenia in Violence
**Status: entirely absent from the web page.**
This is a full named study with its own figures, cited in the book's reference list (and visible
in-text in the "Relationship between specific mental disorders and crime" sub-section of the
"2. Specific Crimes" NoteBlock, book 1 p.3) but never surfaced anywhere in the guide, including
section 10 ("Schizophrenia, Psychosis & Substance Misuse — Violence Risk"), which is exactly where
it belongs:
- Fazel & Grann (2006): population attributable risk fraction of schizophrenia in causing violence
  is **2.3%**, rising to **5%** if psychosis is considered more broadly.
- This attributable risk fraction was **higher in women than men** across all age bands.
- It was **lowest in the 15–24 age band, at 2.5%**.
- In countries with more liberal gun laws, the risk attributable to psychosis for homicide is
  lower — though some argue schizophrenia is responsible for **5–10% of homicides irrespective of
  the baseline homicide rate** (Wallace et al. 2004).
- Wallace et al. (2004) separately estimated that **6–11% of all violent convictions** are
  attributable to schizophrenia.
This is a classic "population attributable risk vs relative risk" distinction the exam likes to
test against the MacArthur/individual-risk figures the guide already covers well — right now a
student would only see the individual-risk side of the argument (1:3000, RR 3.6→25x, etc.) and
never the population-level attributable-risk side.

### 3. Homicide (book 1, p.3)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing:**
- The Swedish-study breakdown of homicide-offender diagnosis is not reproduced: of homicide
  offenders, **9% had schizophrenia, 12% other psychotic disorders, 54% had a secondary
  personality-disorder diagnosis**. The guide only states the aggregate "10% of homicide convicts
  have an abnormal mental state (2/3 psychotic)" and drops this more granular breakdown.
- **10% of homicide MDOs had contact with mental health services within the past year** — the
  book explicitly contrasts this with suicide ("cf. suicide — which has a much higher rate"),
  making it a natural comparison trap. Absent from the web page entirely.

### 4. Filicide (book 1, p.3)
**Status: reasonably covered (D'Orban table/mnemonic present) but missing:**
- The book notes an alternative filicide sub-classification system exists alongside D'Orban's:
  **altruistic, psychotic, accidental, unwanted, and spousal-revenge-related** filicide. The web
  page presents D'Orban's six types as if it were the only classification system in the source —
  this second, unnamed-author system is never mentioned, even as a one-line aside.

### 5. Arson & Fire-Setting (book 1, p.3)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing a table and a definitional nuance:**
- **A complete three-column motive table (adapted from Burton et al., J Am Acad Psychiatry Law
  40:355–65, 2012) is missing entirely.** It classifies fire-setting motives into: **Primary
  gain** (relief from boredom/tension, or excitement including sexual pleasure), **Secondary
  gain** (financial/insurance claim/housing, extortion, to conceal/facilitate another crime), and
  **Other motives** (accidents, carelessness, curiosity — especially in children, delusional
  motive, revenge, jealousy, vandalism, self-destruction/suicide, political protest,
  heroism/recognition, seeking attention). This is a ready-made EMI-matching table and doesn't
  appear anywhere in the guide, which only carries the separate Faulk Group I/II table.
- The book distinguishes **"pathological fire-setters"** (2+ occasions, preoccupation with fire
  but *no* excitement, often no clear motive) from **pyromania proper** (2+ occasions, *with*
  relief of tension/excitement) before giving the DSM-IV pyromania criteria. The web page only
  presents the DSM-IV criteria as if it covers all recurrent fire-setting — this "pathological
  fire-setting without the affective/excitement component" category is dropped, which is exactly
  the kind of near-miss distinction MRCPsych likes to test.

### 6. Sexual Offending — Reoffending, Predictors & the Abel Study (book 1, p.3)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing:**
- A second, distinct named citation on overall reoffending is missing: Grubin & Wingate (1996) —
  "less than one in five of a general sample of sex offenders released from prison go on to
  commit a further sex offence." The guide only cites Hanson et al. (2009)'s "<10% over 6 years";
  Grubin & Wingate's "<1 in 5" figure (a different citation, a different implied timeframe) is a
  plausible distractor/companion figure that never appears.
- Specific figure dropped: **71% of those whose first sexual conviction was against a child
  repeated their sexual offences against children only** (i.e. child-offence specialisation is
  itself quantified in the book — not just "lowest reconviction risk is incest").
- Missing: reconviction rate was **higher when the index offence was against a stranger rather
  than a known adult** (for offenders against adults).
- Missing: **risk of sexual recidivism is higher for homosexual and extra-familial offenders than
  for heterosexual and incest offenders** — a specific comparison the book states plainly and the
  guide never reproduces in this framing (it only covers the boys-vs-girls victim comparison).
- Two extra Lievore (2004) predictors are dropped from the otherwise-thorough list already in the
  guide: **more deviant sexual practices are seen with relatively younger victims**, and
  **psychological maladjustment (substance use/abuse, antisocial attitudes, personality
  disorders)**.

### 7. Domestic Violence (book 1, p.3)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing two minor details:**
- "Men were less likely to tell [anyone] than women" — the book states this explicitly as a sex
  comparison; absent from the guide.
- "The police often come to know about incidents from someone other than the victim" — dropped.

### 8. Prison Psychiatry — Morbidity, Suicide & Self-Harm (book 2, p.8)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing the section's own headline statistic:**
- **"Over 90% of prisoners have a mental disorder"** — this is the very first sentence of the
  book's Prison Psychiatry section and does not appear anywhere in section 19 of the guide, which
  otherwise correctly reproduces the 37/63/57/76% ICD-10-diagnosis breakdown by sub-group. A
  student reading only the web page would miss the single most quotable top-line figure in the
  whole prison-psychiatry topic.
- Also missing: "many prisoners have numeracy and literacy problems, and most prisoners have a
  lower-than-average IQ" (HMIP 1997b/2000a; Singleton et al. 1998) — a supporting fact for the
  above headline that's also absent.

### 9. Special Offender Groups — Women (book 1, p.21)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing several named/quantified findings:**
- A 1953-birth-cohort study (unnamed in-text, but a specific longitudinal finding): followed to
  age 46, **1/3 of men and 9% of women** had been convicted at least once; among these, **8% of
  men were responsible for 2/3 of all crimes**. None of this appears in the guide.
- National Confidential Inquiry into Suicide and Homicide (2003): **55% of homicide victims were
  young men**, and in only **3% of UK homicides were both perpetrator and victim female**. Absent.
- **A quarter of all women in prison had been sentenced for drug offences** — missing.
- For a first-time violent offence, men and women have a similar chance of conviction, but **with
  repeated violence, women have a lower chance of receiving a custodial sentence** — missing.
- **Over half of women in prison are estimated to be mothers** — missing.
- Reoffending-rate comparison with a genuine (and counter-intuitive) trap built in: 2006-cohort
  reoffending rates were similar/slightly lower for women (**46.6% men vs 45.0% women**), but
  women who *do* reoffend are **more prolific** — **445.0 offences per 100 male reoffenders vs
  485.8 per 100 female reoffenders**. The guide has none of this — it would be an easy trap to set
  ("women reoffend less often, therefore less seriously" is exactly the wrong inference the book's
  own figures argue against).
- HMIP also reported the number of self-harm/behavioural *incidents* recorded for women is
  **four times higher than for men** (distinct from, and in addition to, the already-covered "19%
  attempted suicide in the year before custody, ~3x the male rate").

### 10. Psychiatric Legal Defences — Overview Framework (book 2, p.15)
**Status: entirely absent as an organising concept (the underlying content is covered piecemeal).**
The book opens this whole topic with a three-stage timing framework that the guide never states
explicitly: defences differ by **when** in the process they apply — **before trial**: fitness to
plead; **during trial**: not guilty by reason of insanity, infanticide, automatism, diminished
responsibility; **during sentencing**: psychiatric mitigation. Every individual defence is well
covered in the guide's later sections (22–25), but this stage-based classification itself (a
natural EMI stem — "match the defence to the stage of proceedings at which it applies") is never
surfaced anywhere on the page.

### 11. McNaughten Rules (book 2, p.16)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing a definitional nuance that is a classic trap:**
- The book explicitly parenthesises component 3 of the McNaughten test as concerning the
  **physical** nature and quality of the act, distinct from component 4 (the **moral/legal**
  wrongness of the act): "loss of appreciation of nature and quality of an act (the offence;
  physical nature not moral)." The guide's paraphrase ("loss of appreciation of the nature/quality
  of the act") drops this physical-vs-moral distinction, which is precisely the kind of
  near-identical-sounding-criteria trap MRCPsych likes to test between components 3 and 4.

### 12. Automatism (book 2, p.16)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing a confirmed disposal-flowchart nuance.**
Book 2 p.16/17 contains an actual flowchart (verified directly against the PDF, not just the
extracted text, since the diagram initially looked like it might not have survived extraction
cleanly) laying out disposal by outcome: an automatism plea that succeeds as **"insane"** leads to
not-guilty-by-reason-of-insanity, which for **homicide specifically** means an **indefinite stay
in a psychiatric unit**, whereas for **other (non-homicide) offences** it means a **range of
disposals under the Criminal Procedure Act**; a plea that succeeds as **"sane"** always leads to
**unqualified acquittal** regardless of offence type. The guide's sane-vs-insane automatism
comparison table only states the generic "not guilty by reason of insanity — psychiatric disposal
available" for the insane column — it doesn't carry this homicide-vs-other-offences split, which
is a natural EMI stem (match automatism outcome + offence type to disposal).

### 13. Violence Risk Assessment Tools (book 2, p.10)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing one comparative nuance:**
- "There are hardly any actuarial tools available for self-harm and suicide risk, but there are
  many available for violence risk assessment." This asymmetry (actuarial tools are far more
  developed for violence than for self-harm/suicide) is stated plainly in the book and is absent
  from section 21 of the guide, which lists violence/sexual-offending tools (HCR-20, VRAG,
  Static-99, SORAG, SVR-20, SARA) without ever noting that an equivalent suite doesn't really
  exist for suicide/self-harm.

### 14. Secure Settings & Care Models (book 2, p.5)
**Status: reasonably covered but missing one small concept:**
- "Community Forensic Teams" are described in the book as emphasising a **step-down in physical
  and procedural security while stepping up therapeutic care** as a distinct operating principle
  from the three named care models (Parallel/Integrated/Hybrid) that the guide does cover in
  full. This specific team/principle is not named anywhere in section 18.

## Numerical Cross-Check

Every figure in the current guide that could be traced to a specific book sentence was checked
against the literal book text word-for-word (Fazel & Danesh prison figures, MacArthur study
figures, PD-prevalence table, HCR-20/VRAG item lists, Gunn/Maden ICD-10 morbidity table, PD-primary
vs schizophrenia-primary homicide-disposal split, 1:3000/1:33,000 schizophrenia-homicide risk,
psychosis-in-prison 10x/20x figures, etc.). **No numerical discrepancies were found** — every
figure currently on the page matches its source page exactly. The gaps identified above are all
omissions (whole facts/tables/citations never extracted into the guide), not corruptions of
figures that are already there.

---

## Overall Verdict

Forensic Psychiatry is one of the more thorough guides in this corpus — 27 sections with dense
trap/mnemonic/table coverage and clean numeric fidelity throughout. It does not need a rebuild.
It needs a targeted top-up: one entirely missing named study (Fazel & Grann 2006 on population
attributable risk), one missing table (the Burton et al. 2012 arson-motive table), a handful of
specific headline statistics dropped during condensation (most importantly "over 90% of prisoners
have a mental disorder," the women-offenders reoffending-prolificacy contrast, and the 1953-cohort/
National Confidential Inquiry findings), and a couple of classification frameworks mentioned in
the book's own framing language (the three approaches to studying MI/violence; the
before-trial/during-trial/sentencing defence-timing structure) that would cost little to add and
close off some very plausible EMI/SBA stems. Call it roughly eight to ten new
blocks/table-rows/facts spread across five or six existing sections, not a rewrite.
